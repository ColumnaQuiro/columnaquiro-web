## Three weeks out, you cannot get fitter. You can get hurt.

The Valencia Marathon Trinidad Alfonso Zurich is on **Sunday 6 December 2026**. The first wave goes at 8:15 a.m. and the cut-off is 5 hours 30 minutes from the moment you cross the start line ([official regulations](https://www.valenciaciudaddelrunning.com/en/marathon/marathon-regulations/)). It is a flat, fast course that people travel a long way to run, which is exactly why a sore knee in late November feels like a catastrophe: the flights are booked and the hotel is paid for.

It helps to know how ordinary this is. A systematic review put the incidence of lower-limb injury in long-distance runners at anywhere between 19% and 79% depending on the study, with the knee the most common site ([van Gent et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2006.033548)). Late in a training block is when months of accumulated load tend to surface.

Before anything else, the things that are not up for negotiation:

- **Pain at one specific spot on a bone** (shin, foot, hip) that is getting worse, makes you limp, or aches in bed at night. Stop running and see a doctor. That pattern fits a bone stress injury, and running on one makes it worse ([OrthoInfo, AAOS](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/stress-fractures/)).
- **Chest pain, dizziness or breathlessness** out of proportion to the effort. That is for a doctor, not a blog. In Spain the emergency number is 112.
- **A joint that is swollen, locking or giving way.**

None of those is fixed by an adjustment, and nobody credible should try.

## What the evidence says about tapering

Tapering, the planned drop in training load before a race, is one of the few parts of training with reasonably clear data behind it. A meta-analysis of 27 studies in competitive athletes found the most effective approach was a taper of about two weeks in which **volume falls progressively by 41 to 60%, with intensity and frequency left alone** ([Bosquet et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0)).

For recreational runners there is something closer to home. An analysis of training logs from more than 158,000 recreational marathoners found that strict tapers of up to three weeks were associated with faster finishes: a median saving of about five and a half minutes compared with a minimal taper ([Smyth and Lawlor, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2021.735220)). It is observational, so it shows association and not cause, but it points the same way.

In practice: **fewer kilometres, the same running days, the same pace in your quality sessions.** The two classic mistakes are refusing to cut back for fear of losing fitness, and stopping altogether.

## Week by week

**16 to 22 November: start coming down.** Your last proper long run should be behind you. Trim the distance, keep your usual days, keep a little marathon pace. This is not the week to make up the sessions you missed in October. If something has been grumbling, get it looked at now, at home, by someone who knows you. There is still time to adjust your load and see how you respond.

**23 to 29 November: come down properly.** The drop should be obvious this week. Feeling odd is common: heavy legs, aches that come and go. A niggle that moves around and does not get worse as you run is rarely an injury. One that sits in the same spot and grows deserves attention.

**30 November to 6 December: change nothing.** Short easy runs, a few brief stretches at race pace, and nothing new. No new shoes, no new insoles, no stretching routine you have never done, no treatment you have never had. If you are flying in, do not read much into how your legs feel straight off the plane; an easy jog the next morning tells you more. Bib collection is at the Expo at Feria Valencia, on the edge of the city, and it means a fair amount of standing and walking. Go, collect, leave, and make it the only thing on your feet on Saturday.

## The four classic late-cycle niggles

### Outside of the knee: iliotibial band

ITB syndrome is the most common running injury on the outer side of the knee, with an estimated incidence of 5 to 14%. The research on managing it is poor quality and contradictory; what appears to help is work on hip and knee coordination, hip strengthening, mobilisation, and advice on shoes and running surface ([van der Worp et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.2165/11635400-000000000-00000)). You will not rebuild your running form in three weeks. You can take out the downhills and shorten your runs, since it usually comes on after a certain time on your feet. If the pain is elsewhere in the knee, our guide to [knee pain](/en/blog/gonalgia-understanding-knee-pain/) covers the other patterns.

### Heel and arch: plantar fascia

The giveaway is pain with the first steps out of bed. The American Physical Therapy Association's clinical guideline gives its strongest grade of recommendation to manual therapy for the joints and soft tissues of the lower limb, and to plantar fascia and calf stretching, the latter for short-term relief ([Koc et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2023.0303)). Of the four problems here, this is the one where hands-on work has the firmest backing.

### Shin: shin splints, or something more serious

These need telling apart. Diffuse soreness along the inner edge of the shin is usually medial tibial stress syndrome, for which, frankly, no treatment has been shown to work in good-quality trials ([Winters et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0087-0)). What is left is cutting load. Pain **at one point on the bone that is tender when you press it** is a different matter: a bone stress injury moves along a continuum from stress reaction to stress fracture if you keep loading it ([Warden et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2014.5334)). If you are unsure, a doctor and imaging come before a race bib.

### Low back and buttock: sacroiliac joint

One-sided pain low in the back that spreads into the buttock can come from the [sacroiliac joint](/en/blog/solutions-for-inflamed-sacroiliac-joint/). Tests that claim to judge its position or movement are unreliable. The ones that hold up are pain-provocation tests, and it takes three or more positives to point to the joint with reasonable confidence ([Laslett, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1179/jmt.2008.16.3.142)). For low back pain in general, the UK's NICE guideline supports considering manual therapy, but only as part of a package that includes exercise ([NICE NG59](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59)).

## What you can run through, and what you cannot

There is no rule validated for marathoners in a taper. The one most clinicians borrow comes from a trial in Achilles tendinopathy, where runners who kept training did no worse than those who rested, provided they stayed inside a limit ([Silbernagel et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546506298279)): **pain no higher than 5 out of 10, settled by the next morning, and not climbing from one week to the next.** Applying it to other soft-tissue niggles is an extrapolation, so treat it as a guide.

Usually fine to run on, with reduced load: an ache that eases as you warm up, morning stiffness that clears in minutes, general muscle soreness.

Not fine: pain that changes your stride, pain that builds with every kilometre, pinpoint bone pain, any night pain, and swelling.

One more thing. **Do not mask pain with anti-inflammatories to get round the course.** At the Bonn Marathon, runners who took painkillers before the start had almost five times the rate of adverse events, and all nine hospital admissions recorded (kidney failure, bleeds and heart attacks) were in that group ([Küster et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2012-002090)).

## When to see a chiropractor, and when it is too late

First, what we do not offer: an adjustment will not make you faster. A systematic review published in the profession's own journal concluded that spinal manipulation does not improve performance in adults without symptoms ([Corso et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12998-019-0246-y)).

What a runner with a niggle can get from us is an examination that separates the mechanical from what a doctor needs to see, hands-on work where it has support, and an outside opinion on load. There is more on our page for [athletes](/en/who-is-chiropractic-for/athletes/).

On timing: around half of people notice mild, short-lived soreness after a manual therapy session ([Carnes et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.math.2009.12.006)). It is not serious, but it is not what you want to find out the day before a marathon. Our rule of thumb, which is clinical judgement and not a study result:

- **Never been adjusted before?** Race week, in a city you have just landed in, is the wrong moment for a first. If something flares up after you arrive, we can examine it and tell you honestly whether it is a doctor's problem, a run-on-it problem or a do-not-start problem, without adjusting.
- **Already under regular care at home?** Have your last session there, early in race week, with nothing new.
- **Living in Valencia?** This week or next is the window for a first visit.

## The first 48 hours after the finish

A marathon causes real muscle damage. In calf biopsies taken from marathon runners, fibre abnormalities peaked between one and three days after the race and were still there at seven ([Hikida et al., 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6854349/)). Going downstairs sideways on Monday is physiology, not injury.

- **Move, without training.** Walking counts as active recovery, which reduces soreness. Valencia is flat, so Monday's sightseeing is a reasonable plan.
- **Gentle massage, if you fancy it.** In a meta-analysis of 99 studies it was the most effective technique for soreness and perceived fatigue; compression garments and water immersion also helped ([Dupuy et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2018.00403)).
- **Leave adjustments and deep work for later.** With muscle in that state there is no rush, and a few days also show you which pain was soreness and which was not.
- **Watch your urine.** Dark, tea- or cola-coloured urine together with muscle pain far beyond what you would expect, or marked weakness, are signs of rhabdomyolysis and need medical attention straight away ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/rhabdo/signs-symptoms/index.html)). Do not wait until you are home.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can any runner benefit from chiropractic care?**
It depends what for. If you have a mechanical problem in your back, pelvis or foot, an assessment and hands-on work may help. If nothing hurts and you want to run faster, the evidence does not support an adjustment doing that.

**Is it safe to get adjusted right before the race?**
If you are under regular care and know how you respond, a session early in race week is reasonable. If it would be your first, no: short-lived soreness after a session is common, and three days before the start is a bad time to meet it.

**I am flying in on Thursday or Friday. Should I book something on arrival?**
Not as a routine. If you are well, the best thing for your legs is an easy jog and an early night. Book only if something actually hurts and you want it examined before you decide whether to start.

**A pain has appeared two weeks out. Do I have to stop?**
Not necessarily. Cut back for a few days and watch it: if it stays at or below 5 out of 10, settles by the next morning and is not growing, it can usually be managed. If it is on one point of a bone, makes you limp or hurts at night, stop and see a doctor.

**Will I lose fitness by cutting my mileage this much?**
The data say the opposite: reducing volume by 41 to 60% while keeping intensity is associated with better performance, not worse.

**Can I take ibuprofen to get through the race?**
We would not, and the Bonn Marathon data explain why. If you need a painkiller to reach the start line, the real question is whether you should start, and that one is for a doctor.
