**Disc dehydration** is the loss of water from the discs between your vertebrae. It can't be "cured" in the sense of getting your 20-year-old discs back, but **it usually doesn't need to be**: it's a very common finding on scans of people with no pain at all, and when there is pain, most people improve with movement, exercise and time.

## What disc dehydration is

Each intervertebral disc has a gel-like centre (the nucleus pulposus) surrounded by a tough fibrous ring. When you're young, the nucleus is mostly water, which lets it spread load like a cushion. Over the years it loses water and a little height. On an MRI it shows up as a darker disc (a "black disc"), and the report may call it *disc desiccation*, *loss of signal* or *degenerative disc disease*.

## Is it serious? What scans of people without pain show

Usually not. A review of more than 3,000 MRI scans of people **with no back pain at all** found signs of disc degeneration in 37% of 20-year-olds and 96% of 80-year-olds (Brinjikji et al., *American Journal of Neuroradiology*, 2015). It's about as common as grey hair.

So if your report says "disc dehydration at L4-L5" or "L5-S1", that alone doesn't explain your pain or mean your back is "broken". What matters is how you move, what hurts and what makes it worse.

## Symptoms

Many people with dehydrated discs **have no symptoms**. When they do, the most common are:

- Morning **stiffness** that eases once you get moving.
- **Pain** in the lower back or neck that gets worse after sitting or standing in one position for a long time.
- Less tolerance for lifting or bending.
- If the disc also bulges or herniates and irritates a nerve root, pain down the leg (sciatica) or arm, with tingling.

## Causes and what speeds it up

Age is the main factor, and genetics play a big part. More disc degeneration is also linked to:

- **Smoking**, which reduces blood supply to the tissues around the disc.
- **Inactivity** and long hours of sitting.
- **Repeated overload**, such as frequent lifting with poor technique.
- **Excess weight**.

Drinking more water doesn't rehydrate your discs: the water in a disc depends on its structure and on the loading and unloading it gets as you move, not on how many glasses you drink.

## Can disc dehydration be cured?

The disc won't go back to how it was, but it can be **looked after and made more resilient**. A study of runners found that people who ran regularly had better-hydrated, taller lumbar discs than inactive people (Belavý et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2017). Discs are fed by movement: moderate, repeated loading helps; long periods of rest don't.

The realistic goal is **less pain and better function**, and most people get there without surgery.

## Exercises that help

Always within what you can tolerate, without pushing into pain:

- **Walk** every day, starting with 20-30 minutes and building up gradually. This is the foundation.
- **Trunk strengthening**: front and side planks, the "bird dog" (on all fours, extend the opposite arm and leg) and glute bridges.
- **Gentle mobility**: cat-camel on all fours and, if it feels good, gentle press-ups lying face down on your forearms.
- **Swimming or cycling** if walking isn't enough.
- **Break up sitting**: get up every 30-45 minutes, even for a minute.

Mild discomfort that settles within 24 hours is fine. If an exercise leaves you worse the next day, reduce it or swap it.

## How we treat it at the clinic

At your first visit we assess how each segment of your spine moves, which movements hurt and what your day asks of your back. Treatment combines:

- **Gentle adjustments and mobilisation** to restore movement to the stiffest areas.

## When to see a doctor first

See your doctor, or go to A&E if it comes on suddenly, if you notice:

- **Loss of bladder or bowel control**, or numbness around the genitals or between the legs.
- **Weakness** that's getting worse in a leg or arm (your foot drops, you drop things).
- Pain with **fever**, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer.
- Pain that nothing changes and that wakes you every night.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is disc dehydration the same as a herniated disc?**
No. Dehydration is the disc losing water; a herniation is part of the nucleus pushing out through the ring. A dehydrated disc can herniate, but most don't. More in [herniated disc](/en/symptoms/herniated-disc/).

**What does "disc dehydration at L5-S1" mean?**
That the disc between the last lumbar vertebra and the sacrum has lost water. It's the level where it's seen most, because it carries the most load.

**Can I play sport?**
Yes, and it's a good idea. If a particular sport causes pain, adjust how much you do rather than stopping altogether.

**Does drinking more water help?**
Not for your discs. Staying hydrated is good in general, but it won't change what the scan shows.

**Will it get worse?**
The process moves slowly with age, but your pain doesn't have to. Many people with dehydrated discs live without pain.
