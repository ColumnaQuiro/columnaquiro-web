A **cervical disc protrusion** is a disc in the neck that bulges slightly backwards without its nucleus breaking out. It's **very common on scans of people with no pain**, so it isn't always the cause of what you feel. When it does irritate a nerve root, it causes neck pain that travels down the arm, sometimes with tingling. Most cases improve without surgery within weeks or a few months.

## Protrusion, herniation, bulge: what your report means

- **Bulge**: the disc extends broadly and evenly beyond its normal edge. Very common with age.
- **Protrusion**: one area sticks out, but the base of what sticks out is wider than its tip. The outer ring still holds the nucleus in.
- **Extrusion** or **herniation**: part of the nucleus breaks through the ring.

In a study of 1,211 people **with no symptoms**, 87.6% had some cervical disc bulging on MRI, from their 20s onwards (Nakashima et al., *Spine*, 2015). So what matters is whether the scan **fits your symptoms and your examination**.

## Symptoms

- **Neck pain** and stiffness, sometimes with pain between the shoulder blades.
- **Pain down the arm** if it irritates a nerve root (cervical radiculopathy).
- **Tingling or numbness** in specific parts of the hand.
- **Weakness** in some arm or hand movements.
- Worse looking up or tilting your head towards the painful side; resting your **hand on top of your head sometimes eases it**.

## C5-C6 and C6-C7: why the level matters

These are the levels most often affected, because they move the most:

- **C5-C6** (C6 root): pain and sometimes tingling towards the **thumb and index finger**, with possible weakness of the biceps and wrist.
- **C6-C7** (C7 root): towards the **middle finger**, with possible triceps weakness (pushing, straightening the elbow).
- **C4-C5** (C5 root): more towards the **shoulder** and upper arm.

## Causes and risk factors

- **Age-related disc changes**, the main factor.
- **Long hours with your head forward** at screens.
- **Jobs** with the neck bent or loads carried on the head and shoulders.
- **Injuries**, such as whiplash.
- **Smoking**, linked to more disc degeneration.

## Outlook: most people improve without surgery

Symptoms of cervical radiculopathy usually improve a lot in the first few weeks, and most people recover with conservative care. Surgery is kept for weakness that's getting worse, compression of the spinal cord, or pain that hasn't improved after months of good treatment.

## What you can do at home

- **Stay active** and move your neck within what you can tolerate; a collar isn't recommended unless a doctor prescribes one.
- **Heat** on the neck and shoulders for stiffness; ice if the pain is very sharp and recent.
- **Chin tucks**: draw your chin straight back without dropping your head, hold for 5 seconds, 10 repetitions several times a day, as long as the arm pain doesn't increase.
- **Ergonomics**: screen at eye level, phone at face height, frequent breaks.
- **Sleep** on a pillow that keeps your neck straight, on your side or back.

A good sign: if the pain **retreats from your arm towards your neck** (centralisation), you're heading the right way even if your neck still aches.

## How we treat it at the clinic

We examine strength, sensation and reflexes in your arms to work out which nerve root is involved and rule out spinal cord compression. If conservative care is appropriate:

- **Gentle mobilisation** of the neck and upper back. With arm symptoms we **don't use forceful neck manipulation**.
- **Progressive exercises** for neck control and shoulder and upper-back strength.
- **Advice** on work, screens and sleep.

We review your progress at every visit and, if the neurological signs don't improve or get worse, we refer you to a doctor.

## When to see a doctor first

See a doctor promptly if you notice:

- **Weakness** in your arm or hand that's getting worse.
- **Clumsiness in both hands** (buttons, writing), **unsteadiness when walking** or changes in bladder control: these can point to spinal cord compression (myelopathy).
- Symptoms in **both arms** or in your legs.
- Pain after an **accident or blow**, or with fever, weight loss or a history of cancer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a protrusion the same as a herniated disc?**
Not exactly. In a protrusion the disc bulges but its nucleus stays contained; in a herniation (extrusion) part of the nucleus escapes. In practice, symptoms and treatment can be very similar. See [herniated disc](/en/symptoms/herniated-disc/).

**Do I need an MRI?**
Not always. It's requested if there's weakness, warning signs, or no improvement after several weeks of treatment.

**How long does it take to heal?**
Many people feel clearly better within 4-6 weeks; some take a few months. Tingling is often the last thing to go.

**Can I exercise?**
Yes, with adjustments. At first, avoid things that load the neck heavily (overhead pressing, contact sports, very low road-cycling positions) until you improve.

**Can it cause dizziness or headaches?**
It can cause headaches that start at the back of the neck. Dizziness is less typical and worth checking with your doctor. See [neck pain and headaches](/en/symptoms/neck-and-headache-pain/).
