**Cervical lordosis** is the natural, gentle C-shaped curve of the neck. When that curve is partly or fully lost and the vertebrae line up straighter, it is called a **straightened neck** (in Spain, *rectificación cervical*). It is a very common X-ray finding and, while it can go with neck pain and stiffness, it isn't always the cause: many people with a straight neck have no symptoms at all.

## What cervical lordosis is

The seven vertebrae of the neck form a curve that helps spread the weight of your head (about 5 kg) and absorb impact. That curve can be:

- **Normal**, with its C shape.
- **Reduced or straightened**, when the neck looks straighter than usual.
- **Increased (hyperlordosis)**, when the curve is more pronounced.

## Why the neck straightens

It usually develops gradually from holding your head forward for long periods, especially looking at a phone or computer. It can also follow whiplash or come with age-related wear. Sometimes the neck simply looks straight on an X-ray because the muscles were tense or the chin was tucked when the image was taken.

## Common symptoms

When a straightened neck does cause symptoms, the most common are:

- Neck stiffness and tiredness at the end of the day.
- Pain spreading to the shoulders and upper back.
- Headaches that start at the base of the skull (see our [guide to headaches from the neck](/en/blog/complete-guide-to-treating-cervical-headache/)).
- Fatigue when holding your head up for a long time.

## Does the curve explain the pain?

Not always. Studies comparing neck shape with neck pain have found no clear link: people with straight necks and no pain, and people with pain and a normal curve, are both very common (Grob et al., *European Spine Journal*, 2007). So we don't treat the X-ray; we treat you: how your neck moves, what hurts and what makes it worse.

## How we treat it

At the clinic we assess how each part of your neck and upper back moves, your working posture and your history. Treatment combines:

- **Gentle adjustments and mobilisation** to restore movement to the stiffest areas.
- **Strengthening exercises** for the deep neck muscles and upper back, which hold your head up all day.
- **Posture advice**: screen height, how you use your phone, and a break every hour.

The goal is less pain and a neck that moves better. Whether the curve changes on a follow-up X-ray is secondary, and we won't ask you for repeat X-rays to check.

## When to see a doctor first

See your doctor if you have tingling or weakness in your arms, dizziness with double vision or trouble speaking, pain after a hard knock or accident, or pain that keeps you awake and doesn't change with position.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is cervical lordosis the same as a straightened neck?**
No. Lordosis is the name of the neck's normal curve. A straightened neck is when that curve is lost.

**Can a straightened neck be reversed?**
The curve may improve a little, but what matters is pain and movement, which usually improve within a few weeks of treatment and exercise.

**How soon will I notice a difference?**
Many people feel less tension within the first few sessions. Making it last with exercises takes several weeks.

**Can I exercise with a straightened neck?**
Yes, and it's a good idea. Focus on strengthening your neck and upper back, and at first avoid positions that hold your neck bent forwards or backwards for a long time.

**What can I do at home?**
Raise your screen to eye level, bring your phone up to your face instead of dropping your head, take a break every hour, and use a pillow that keeps your neck in line.
