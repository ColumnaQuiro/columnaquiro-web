## Starting with the awkward part

**Chiropractic is not a regulated health profession in Spain.** It does not appear in [Ley 44/2003](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2003-21340), the law that defines which professions are: medicine, nursing, physiotherapy, podiatry and the rest.

That has one direct consequence worth understanding before you book anywhere: **there is no official professional body, no state-recognised Spanish qualification, and no public register to check.** Nobody will stop someone putting "quiropráctico" on a sign.

If you have moved here from the UK, Ireland, the US, or a Nordic country, this is probably the biggest surprise. In those countries chiropractic is statutorily regulated — in the UK by the General Chiropractic Council, and you can look a practitioner up in a public register. **None of that infrastructure exists in Spain.** The care can be just as good; the verification is entirely on you.

Some people in this sector would rather not say that out loud. We take the opposite view: if the state is not filtering, you have to, and for that you need to know what to look at.

## What real chiropractic training looks like

The international benchmark exists, even if it is not Spanish. A serious chiropractic programme:

- Runs **five to six years full time** at a higher education institution. Not a course, not a weekend master's, not a 200-hour specialisation.
- Spends the **final years on supervised clinical practice**, treating real patients under supervision.
- Is accredited by the **European Council on Chiropractic Education (ECCE)** in Europe, or its equivalent elsewhere.

That accreditation is the key piece, because it is the only thing separating a chiropractic degree from a diploma with the same name and forty times fewer hours. And it is checkable: you can ask for it.

## What the AEQ is — and is not

This needs stating precisely, because a lot of imprecision circulates.

The **Asociación Española de Quiropráctica (AEQ)** is the main professional association in Spain. To admit someone it requires a university chiropractic degree from an ECCE-recognised institution, expressly rejects part-time "similar" training, asks for documentation from the university, puts experienced practitioners through an examination on chiropractic practice in Spain, and requires annual continuing education.

In practice it is the most demanding quality filter that exists in the country.

**What it is not: a regulator.** The AEQ is a private association, not an official college, and has no state authority to permit or prohibit anyone from practising. You will sometimes see claims that the AEQ "regulates the profession in Spain". That is not accurate: in Spain the profession is not regulated by anyone — and that is precisely why voluntary membership of an association with hard entry requirements tells you something about the person who holds it.

Put differently: AEQ membership is a good sign. Its absence is not illegal. That is the real situation.

## The questions to ask

Call, email, or ask at the first visit. A practitioner who is annoyed by these questions has already answered them:

1. **"Where did you study, and how long was the programme?"** Look for five or six years full time. If the answer is a course, a short postgraduate qualification, or training fitted around another job, it is not the same thing.
2. **"Is the institution you studied at ECCE-accredited?"** And if you want to go further: ask to see the degree.
3. **"Are you a member of the AEQ?"**
4. **"What happens at the first visit?"** It should include a history and an examination before any adjustment. If you get adjusted without being examined, leave.
5. **"How many sessions do you think I will need, and when do we review that?"** An estimated range and a review date. Not a fixed number quoted before seeing you, and not an open-ended "we'll see".
6. **"What happens if I do not improve?"** The right answer involves reassessing and, where appropriate, referring you elsewhere.
7. **"Do you carry professional liability insurance?"** Almost nobody asks this, and it may be the most practical question on the list. With no compulsory professional college, there is no compulsory insurance attached to registration either — carrying it is voluntary. Anyone practising seriously has it, and will have no problem telling you so.

## Red flags

None of these are negotiable. If they show up, go somewhere else:

**Being adjusted at the first visit with no history or examination.** The order matters: first rule out what is not mechanical, then treat. The reverse is reckless.

**A fixed number of sessions quoted before examining you.** "It's 24 sessions" over the phone is a product, not a clinical plan.

**Long prepaid contracts sold at a discount.** Convenient for the clinic, bad for you if four sessions in it turns out not to be for you. Be especially wary of non-refundable ones.

**Promises of a cure.** Nobody serious guarantees an outcome, least of all for conditions outside the musculoskeletal field. If you hear that chiropractic cures asthma, allergies, infertility or depression, you are hearing a claim the evidence does not support.

**X-rays for everyone, as routine.** Not everyone needs imaging, and imaging that adds nothing only adds radiation and cost.

**Being advised against seeing a doctor.** An absolute red line. A good chiropractor sends you to a doctor when there are warning signs; they do not compete with one.

**Maintenance plans presented as compulsory.** Maintenance exists and suits some people, but the evidence behind it is clearly weaker than for treating a specific episode. It is an option you choose, not a condition of being discharged.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it illegal to practise chiropractic in Spain?**
No. It is not regulated as a health profession, which is different from being prohibited. What does not exist is an official Spanish qualification or a compulsory professional college.

**Can anyone call themselves a chiropractor?**
In practice, yes — which is exactly why asking about training is worth it. There are practitioners with six-year degrees and people with a short course using the same word.

**My home country has a chiropractic register. Does that count here?**
Your registration at home does not transfer into any Spanish system, because there is no Spanish system to transfer into. It is still strong evidence of training, so mention it if you are comparing practitioners — and ask a Spanish-based practitioner where they studied instead.

**Are reviews a good guide?**
They tell you about manner and patient experience, but they do not replace training. Both are information; only one tells you what the person adjusting you actually studied.

**Should I worry if my chiropractor is not an AEQ member?**
It is a fair question to ask, and worth listening to the answer. Not belonging does not imply bad practice, but it does mean nobody external has verified their qualification — so in that case you will need to see it yourself.

**What can I do if I think a clinic treated me badly?**
You can complain as a consumer through your regional consumer office, and if the practitioner belongs to the AEQ, to the association as well. If you think you have been harmed, see your doctor first and keep the reports.
