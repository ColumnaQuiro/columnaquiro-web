## The sport nobody warned you about

It usually goes like this. You move to Valencia, a colleague or neighbour adds you to a WhatsApp group, and within a month you are playing padel three evenings a week. You had not played a racket sport since school. Then one morning your lower back is stiff enough that putting on socks takes planning.

You are in large company. Spain ended 2024 with 109,040 federated padel licences, according to the Ministry's [federated sport statistics](https://www.educacionfpydeportes.gob.es/eu/dam/jcr:8ebf13a7-f944-4705-8992-5fecec3ea89c/estadistica-de-deporte-federado-2024.pdf), and licence holders are a small share of the people on court. Most social players, and nearly all newcomers from abroad, never register.

Here is what the research says. A [2023 systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2023-001607) (eight studies, 2,022 players) puts the rate at about 3 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and 8 per 1,000 matches. The elbow is the most commonly injured site, followed by the knee, the shoulder and **the lower back**. In a [sample of 113 recreational players in Spain](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26954572/), 85.4% reported at least one padel injury, with elbow and lower back the two most common locations.

A rough calculation, and it is only that: three matches a week is about 150 a year. At 8 injuries per 1,000 matches, that averages slightly more than one injury a year, anywhere in the body.

The honest caveat: the review's authors point out that the studies are few, small and questionnaire-based. Nobody has pinned down what share of players get back pain from padel, so be wary of any article that gives you a precise percentage.

## First: when it is not a padel problem

Most lower back pain has no serious cause and improves within a few weeks, as the [Lancet low back pain series](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2818%2930480-X) summarises. Some symptoms cannot wait, though. Going by [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/back-pain/), treat it as an emergency if back pain comes with:

- Tingling, weakness or numbness in **both legs**.
- Loss of feeling around the genitals or anus.
- Difficulty peeing, or loss of bladder or bowel control.
- A serious impact beforehand. A hard fall into the glass or the fence counts.

In Spain that means *urgencias* at the nearest hospital, or calling 112. See a doctor soon, without it being an emergency, if you also have a fever or feel generally unwell, have lost weight without trying, or the pain is worse at night and does not ease with rest. None of that is fixed by a warm-up or an adjustment.

## Why the smash loads your lower back and shoulder

If you grew up with tennis or squash, padel looks gentle: small court, underarm serve, slower ball. The catch is the lob. It is the standard way to push opponents off the net, so you hit far more overheads per match than you expect.

Watch a smash in slow motion. To reach a ball that has got behind you, you step back, arch backwards, rotate your trunk and bend to one side. Then all of it reverses in a fraction of a second. Extension, rotation and side-bending together, at speed, is the combination the lower lumbar spine tolerates least well.

No study has yet measured lumbar load in the padel smash. The closest evidence is the tennis serve: in [elite adolescent tennis players](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23470302/), side-bending forces on the lumbar spine during the serve were roughly eight times those of running, and were higher in players with a history of back pain. Elite teenagers are not 45-year-old club players, so treat that as a pointer. For padel itself, a [sports medicine review](https://journal.aspetar.com/en/journals/volume-12-targeted-topic-sports-medicine-in-padel/injuries-in-padel) singles out smashes as the strokes that stress the spine most.

The shoulder part is simple mechanics. Smash power comes from the legs and trunk rotation; the arm passes it on. If your hips or mid-back are stiff, which is common after years at a desk, you have two options: arch the lower back further, or hit with the shoulder alone. Both add up over a season.

Then there is everything else: the half-squat ready position, bending for low balls off the back glass, and hard lateral stops on a court only ten metres wide, where you rarely take more than three steps before braking.

## The asymmetry of always playing the same side

Padel is a one-armed sport, and most of us also play the same side of the court every time. If you are right-handed and play the left side, the high balls down the middle are yours. More smashes, the same rotation, week after week.

The body adapts. In [high-level players](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk11010111), the dominant hand grips with an average of 49 kg against 44 kg for the other, and in [amateur players](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports14100467) the dominant leg produces more force when jumping and landing.

To be straight about it: **nobody has shown that this asymmetry causes back pain.** It is a normal adaptation to a one-sided sport. What is sensible is not letting it grow unchecked: warm up rotating both ways, swap sides in a friendly game now and then, and do some strength work that is not padel. We go into this on our page about [chiropractic for athletes](/en/who-is-chiropractic-for/athletes/).

## A 6-minute routine before the match

More honesty first. No warm-up has been shown in a trial to reduce padel injuries, and the sports medicine review above says so plainly. This is not a validated protocol. It simply rehearses what you are about to ask of your body: rotating, braking and smashing. What certainly prepares nothing is the usual two-minute knock-up with your jacket still on.

Do it beside the court, racket within reach:

1. **Minute 0 to 1: raise your pulse.** Easy jog around the court; spend the last 20 seconds side-stepping in both directions.
2. **Minute 1 to 2: hips.** Ten forward-and-back leg swings each side, one hand on the fence. Then ten hip hinges: hands on hips, long back, push your hips back and return.
3. **Minute 2 to 3: lunge with rotation.** Long step forward, rotate your trunk towards the front leg. Five each side, unhurried. This is the smash rotation, shared between hips and mid-back.
4. **Minute 3 to 4: lateral stops.** Three quick side-steps and a hard stop with the knee bent and chest up. Six in each direction. You will repeat this more than any other movement in the match.
5. **Minute 4 to 5: shoulders.** Ten arm circles forwards, ten backwards. Then twenty seconds of shadow strokes with the racket, including on your non-dominant side.
6. **Minute 5 to 6: shadow smashes.** Eight smashes with no ball: three at half speed, three at 70%, the last two close to full. Think about pushing with your legs and rotating, not arching. Finish with two short sprints to the net.

Then knock up as usual. If you arrive late, three minutes of this beats none.

## When soreness becomes an injury

A tired, heavy back after a hard match is not an injury. Muscle soreness is typical when you start a sport or step up the load sharply, and the sensible response is to [ease off for a day or two](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200333020-00005), not to go to bed.

**It is probably soreness or overload if** the ache is diffuse and on both sides, shows up the next day, eases once you get moving and fades within a few days. Keep active: [low back pain guidelines](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2818%2930489-6) recommend movement and normal activity over rest.

**Get it assessed if:**

- It was a sharp, specific twinge during one stroke, and you have not been able to twist or bend the same way since.
- The pain is clearly one-sided and returns every match, a little earlier each time.
- It travels into the buttock or leg, or you notice tingling.
- You have changed how you smash to avoid it.
- It has not improved after two or three weeks.

If what you feel is a hard, tender band of muscle, our article on [lumbar muscle contractures](/en/blog/lumbar-muscle-contractures/) may help.

## What we can do, and what we cannot

A first visit for back pain starts with a history and examination before any treatment: how your hips, mid-back and lower back move, and which movement reproduces the pain. If anything points away from a mechanical problem, we refer you to a doctor.

For mechanical [low back pain](/en/symptoms/low-back-pain/), the UK's [NICE guideline](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59) lists manual therapy, including spinal manipulation, as an option, but only as part of a package that includes exercise. That is how we use it: an adjustment can help with pain and movement, and what changes the outlook is hips and a trunk that cope with the load you put on them. We cannot promise the pain will not return; recurrence is common with any low back pain. There are also no chiropractic studies specifically in padel players.

What you rarely need to do is stop playing. Dropping from three matches a week to two for a while, smashing less and warming up properly is usually enough.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal for my lower back to hurt after padel?**
Common, which is not the same as inevitable. The lower back is among the four most frequently injured sites in padel. A diffuse ache that clears in a few days is usually overload; pain that comes back every match deserves an assessment.

**Can I keep playing with lower back pain?**
If it is mild, does not travel down the leg and does not worsen during the match, usually yes, at lower intensity and with fewer smashes. If it forces you to change your technique or keeps getting worse, stop and have it checked.

**I played tennis for years. Why does padel hurt my back when tennis never did?**
Possibly the volume of overheads and the constant low, bent position near the glass, combined with a sudden jump from no matches to several a week. There is no study comparing the two directly for back pain, so this is reasoning, not proof.

**Which shot is hardest on the back?**
The smash, especially when you are late and solve it by arching backwards. Letting that ball go and playing a *bandeja* or taking it off the back glass is usually the better choice, for your back and for the point.

**How many matches a week is too many?**
No number is backed by research. The jump matters more than the total: going from one match to three overnight is harder on you than three after a gradual build. Leave at least a day between matches while you adapt.

**When should I see a doctor instead of a chiropractor?**
Leg weakness or numbness, trouble peeing, fever, unexplained weight loss or a heavy impact. That is *urgencias* or your GP (*médico de cabecera*), not a chiropractic appointment.
