## First: when not to wait

A crash abroad is stressful in a way a crash at home is not. You are dealing with a *parte amistoso* in a second language, an insurer you have never phoned, and a neck that may not have started hurting yet. Most of what you will find online about whiplash in Spain is written by injury-claim lawyers. This is not that. It is the clinical side only: what is happening in your neck, what is expected, and what is not.

What is not expected comes first. After a road accident, **go to *urgencias* (A&E) or call 112**, Spain's emergency number, if you notice any of these, even hours or days later:

- **Severe pain** that paracetamol or ibuprofen does not touch.
- **Tingling or pins and needles** on one or both sides of your body.
- **Weakness** in your hands, arms or legs.
- **Trouble walking** or sitting upright.
- A sudden **"electric shock" feeling** in your neck and back.

That is the urgent-review list from the [NHS guidance on whiplash](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/whiplash/). The same page says not to drive or cycle until you have been assessed.

If you also **hit your head**, the list gets longer. Being knocked out, being unable to stay awake, a seizure, problems with vision or hearing, trouble speaking or understanding, loss of balance, or a change in behaviour are all emergency signs in the [NHS head injury guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/head-injury-and-concussion/).

And three situations deserve a doctor's assessment even if you feel more or less fine: being **65 or older**, a **high-energy crash** (a high-speed collision, a rollover, being thrown from the vehicle), or **pins and needles in your arms or legs**. Those are the high-risk factors in the [Canadian C-spine rule](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.286.15.1841), the tool emergency departments use to decide who needs a neck X-ray.

## What whiplash actually is

Whiplash is an injury to the structures of the neck (muscles, ligaments, discs and joints) caused by the head being abruptly accelerated and then stopped, classically in a rear-end collision. On a Spanish discharge report you will not see the word "whiplash". Look for ***latigazo cervical***, ***esguince cervical*** (neck sprain) or ***cervicalgia postraumática***. They all describe the same thing.

Not all whiplash is equal. The grading used since 1995 comes from the [Quebec Task Force](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7604354/):

- **Grade I:** neck pain, stiffness or tenderness, with nothing found on examination.
- **Grade II:** the same plus musculoskeletal signs, such as reduced movement or tender points.
- **Grade III:** neurological signs are added: reduced reflexes, weakness or altered sensation in the arms.
- **Grade IV:** fracture or dislocation.

The overwhelming majority of what follows a low-speed shunt is grade I or II. Grades III and IV are medical territory, not something for a chiropractic clinic.

## Why it hurts the next day, not at the scene

This is the part that unsettles people most. You get out of the car feeling fine, exchange details, drive home, and the next morning you cannot turn your head to reverse out of the garage.

That delay is typical. The NHS notes that symptoms can take several hours to start. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/11982-whiplash) is more specific: some symptoms begin straight away, others take at least 12 hours, and the full picture may need a day or even a few days to show. The [Clínica Universidad de Navarra](https://www.cun.es/diccionario-medico/terminos/latigazo-cervical), a Spanish university hospital, puts the usual delay at 12 to 24 hours and up to 72, and gives two reasons: the inflammatory response in the tissues takes time to build, and adrenaline at the moment of impact masks pain.

Two practical consequences:

1. **Feeling fine at the roadside does not mean nothing happened.** Keep an eye on yourself for the following three days.
2. **Pain that arrives late is not, in itself, a bad sign.** In the Canadian C-spine rule, delayed onset of neck pain actually counts as a low-risk feature for serious injury.

## The full symptom picture

The common ones, as listed by the NHS:

- **Neck pain** and stiffness, with difficulty moving your head.
- **Headaches**.
- **Pain and muscle spasms** in the shoulders and arms.

Our page on [neck pain](/en/symptoms/neck-pain/) goes through where each kind of ache tends to come from. If what you mostly feel is one hard, shortened muscle, the article on [neck contractures](/en/blog/neck-contracture/) covers that.

Then there are the symptoms that frighten people because nobody mentioned them:

**Dizziness and unsteadiness.** Not rare. A paper in the [*European Spine Journal*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00586-005-0970-y) notes vertigo and dizziness are reported in 25 to 50% of cases, and a review in [*Spine*](https://doi.org/10.1097/BRS.0b013e3182387f78) describes dizziness as one of the most frequent complaints among people whose pain persists. We have a separate article on [neck-related vertigo](/en/blog/chiropractic-for-vertigo/).

**Tinnitus (ringing in the ears).** It happens, but the numbers are shakier. A [2009 review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jflm.2008.09.001) estimates that roughly 10% of people with whiplash develop ear-related symptoms: tinnitus, hearing loss or vertigo. Yet a [study of 109 patients](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022215108002569) with low-grade whiplash (grades I and II) found only four with dizziness, all short-lived, and none with lasting tinnitus. The honest reading: it occurs, the figures vary a lot between studies, and in that group of mild cases it was the exception.

**Vision problems, hoarseness or trouble swallowing.** The Cleveland Clinic lists these at the more severe end. If they appear, see a doctor. If vision or hearing changes after a blow to the head, treat it as an emergency.

## Why the collar fell out of favour

For decades the picture of whiplash was a soft foam collar worn for weeks. Current advice runs the other way. The NHS is blunt about it: do not use a neck brace or collar, because **it does not help**, and do not rest your neck for long periods.

You may still walk out of a Spanish emergency department with a *collarín cervical* and instructions to wear it. That is worth a conversation, not a panic, and the evidence helps you have it:

- A [randomised trial of 200 patients](https://doi.org/10.1136/emj.2003.010165) compared collar therapy with physiotherapist-led exercise. At six weeks the exercise group had less pain (1.04 against 1.60 out of 10) and less disability (0.92 against 1.56). The authors' conclusion: early active mobilisation is superior to the collar.
- A [review of cervical collar use](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2684205/) cites a study in which, two years on, 46% of the rest-and-collar group still had symptoms, against 23% of those advised to mobilise early.

The fair caveat: that same review found a soft collar worn for ten days or less has not been shown to cause harm. So a few days in one is not a mistake. The problem is **prolonged** use, because a neck that does not move stiffens and weakens. If a doctor prescribed it for a specific injury (a grade III or IV), follow the doctor, not a blog. Otherwise, ask at your follow-up how soon you can come out of it: *"¿Cuándo puedo quitarme el collarín?"*

## What to do in the first weeks

Once the serious possibilities are ruled out, the NHS advice is short:

- **Carry on with everyday activities** as far as you can. It may hurt a little, but it speeds recovery.
- **Ordinary painkillers** such as paracetamol or ibuprofen if you need them and can take them. Both are sold over the counter in any *farmacia*, and the pharmacist can advise.
- **Avoid long periods of resting** your neck.
- **See a doctor if you have not improved after a week**, if painkillers are not enough, or if the pain worries you. In Valencia that means your *centro de salud* if you are in the public system, or your private insurer's network if you are not.

Keep the *informe de urgencias* you were given. Whoever examines you next will want to read it.

## How long does whiplash last?

The honest answer has two halves.

**The reassuring half:** whiplash usually gets better within **two to three months**, according to the NHS. The Clínica Universidad de Navarra says the same of grades I and II: most recover over weeks to a few months.

**The half that tends to be left out:** some people take longer. The Navarra source puts residual neck pain in the medium term at 20 to 40% of patients. And the most cited review, from the [Bone and Joint Decade Task Force on Neck Pain](https://doi.org/10.1097/brs.0b013e3181643eb8), concluded that about half of people with whiplash still report some neck pain a year after the injury. "Some neck pain" is not the same as being disabled, but it is worth knowing so you do not assume something has gone wrong if you are not at 100% by month three.

That review also identified what predicts a slower recovery: **more pain, more symptoms and more disability at the start**, plus psychological factors such as a passive coping style, low mood and fear of movement. Details of the collision itself, such as the direction of impact or the type of headrest, turned out to predict very little. Put differently: how you respond in the first weeks matters more than how the car looked.

## Where chiropractic fits

With the same frankness: no therapy "cures" whiplash, and nobody can guarantee a timeline.

What does exist is a [2016 clinical practice guideline](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmpt.2016.08.007) which, for recent-onset neck pain of grades I and II, suggests multimodal care: manual therapy (manipulation or mobilisation), self-management advice and exercise. For whiplash specifically, what it suggests is supervised exercise with advice. Manual therapy is therefore one component of an active plan, not a replacement for moving.

One thing to know if you are used to the UK, Ireland or North America: chiropractic is not a regulated health profession in Spain, so checking who you are seeing falls to you. ## Frequently asked questions

**How long after a car accident can whiplash symptoms appear?**
Often 12 to 24 hours later, and sometimes up to 72 hours. That is why it makes sense to monitor yourself for three days after a crash even if nothing hurts at first.

**What is the recovery time for mild whiplash?**
Most people improve over a few weeks to two or three months. A proportion have symptoms for longer, particularly when the initial pain and restriction were greater.

**Should I wear the neck collar I was given?**
As a rule, current guidance discourages collars and prolonged rest for ordinary whiplash and favours early movement. The exception is a collar prescribed by a doctor for a specific injury, in which case follow that advice and ask when you can stop.

**Is dizziness normal after whiplash?**
It is a commonly reported symptom. Even so, dizziness that is severe, comes with problems of vision, hearing, balance or speech, or follows a blow to the head needs urgent medical assessment.

**Can I drive with whiplash?**
Not until you have been assessed if you have any of the warning signs. If you have none, the practical test is whether you can turn your head far enough to check safely. If you cannot, do not drive.

**Where do I go in Valencia after a crash if I do not speak much Spanish?**
For anything on the warning list, hospital *urgencias* or 112. For symptoms that are not urgent, your *centro de salud* or your private insurer's clinics. Useful words: *latigazo cervical* (whiplash), *hormigueo* (tingling), *mareo* (dizziness), *pitidos en el oído* (ringing in the ears).

**When can I see a chiropractor after an accident?**
Once a doctor has ruled out serious injury, or when there are no warning signs and your symptoms fit a grade I or II picture. With tingling, weakness or severe pain, the first stop is *urgencias*.
