## Two appointments, half the problem each

If you have moved to Valencia with a jaw that aches in the morning and a neck that never quite loosens, you have probably already done the rounds. A dentist looked at your molars, said *bruxismo* and proposed a *férula de descarga*. Someone else worked on your neck. Both did their job properly, and you still left without an answer to the obvious question: why do the two hurt together?

This post is that answer. It covers how the jaw and upper neck are linked, what each professional can realistically do, and where the research is solid and where it is thin. The short version: **a night guard and neck care are not rivals. They cover different parts of the problem**, and neither is a guaranteed fix.

## First, when jaw pain is not a jaw problem

Jaw pain does not always come from the joint. None of the following should wait for a dental or chiropractic appointment:

- **Chest pain or pressure spreading to the jaw, neck or arm**, with breathlessness, sweating or nausea. This can be a [heart attack](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-attack/symptoms/). In Spain the emergency number is 112, not 999 or 911.
- **Jaw pain when chewing together with a severe headache, a tender temple or scalp, or any change in vision.** That pattern fits [temporal arteritis](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/temporal-arteritis/), which needs urgent medical care because it can damage eyesight.
- **You cannot open or close your mouth**, or the jaw has locked.
- **Facial swelling, fever or pain in one specific tooth**, which points to a dental infection.
- **Pain after a blow** to the face or jaw.

If none of that sounds like you, read on.

## What is actually hurting

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ, or *ATM* in Spanish) is the hinge just in front of each ear. Problems with the joint or the muscles that move it are grouped as **temporomandibular disorders**, or TMD.

They are common, though the number depends on what you count. A [systematic review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00784-020-03710-w) found signs of a joint disorder in roughly 31% of adults when they were examined against diagnostic criteria. Most of those people are not in pain: the [2023 BMJ clinical guideline](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-076227) puts chronic TMD pain at 6 to 9% of adults.

Two points that take a lot of the worry out:

- **Clicking without pain is normal.** The US National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research is blunt about it: painless joint sounds are common and [do not need treatment](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/tmd).
- **Bruxism is not automatically a disorder.** The [2018 international consensus](https://doi.org/10.1111/joor.12663) defines it as a jaw-muscle behaviour (clenching or grinding, asleep or awake), not a disease in itself in otherwise healthy people. It can be a risk factor, but plenty of people grind and feel nothing.

## How the jaw and the neck talk to each other

This is the part that tends to get skipped. There are three pieces, from most to least solid.

**They move together.** Opening your mouth is not a jaw-only movement. A [classic study](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345000790060501) recorded healthy adults chewing and found that the head extends in step with each jaw opening. The authors concluded that the jaw and neck motor systems are functionally integrated. It was a small study of twelve people, but you can check the idea yourself: open wide and you will feel your head tip back slightly.

**They share wiring.** Pain signals from the face and jaw travel in the trigeminal nerve; those from the upper neck travel in the first three cervical nerves. Both [converge on the same neurons](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11916-001-0029-7) in the brainstem. That convergence is the accepted explanation for neck problems being felt as headache, and the leading hypothesis for why jaw and neck pain blur into each other. We go into it in our [guide to headaches that come from the neck](/en/blog/complete-guide-to-treating-cervical-headache/).

**They show up together in studies.** A [meta-analysis of observational studies](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm9092806) found a clinically relevant association between neck disability and jaw disability in people with TMD, along with reduced neck movement and greater pressure sensitivity.

Now the limit, which matters: **association is not cause.** These studies cannot tell you whether the neck loads the jaw, the jaw loads the neck, or both are responding to the same thing. That same meta-analysis also found **no difference in head posture** between people with and without TMD. If you have been told your jaw hurts "because of forward head posture", the evidence does not back that up cleanly.

## Why a night guard alone often is not enough

A night guard is designed to put a barrier between your teeth and protect them while you clench. If your dentist sees wear, wearing one makes sense, and nothing here is an argument for dropping it.

What the research does not confirm is that a splint on its own takes the pain away. The [largest UK review](https://doi.org/10.3310/hta24070) concluded that the available evidence, rated very low quality, did not show that splints reduce pain in TMD. The BMJ guideline points the same way: a conditional recommendation **against** reversible splints as a treatment for chronic TMD pain, and a strong recommendation against irreversible ones, meaning those that permanently change your bite.

Three reasonable explanations for why it falls short:

1. **It works at night.** If you clench during the day, at a laptop or in traffic on the V-30, the guard is in its case.
2. **It protects the tooth, not the habit.** The muscles can keep working against the plastic.
3. **It leaves everything else alone.** Your neck, your sleep, and [stress](/en/symptoms/stress-and-depression/), which tends to make the pain worse. Relocating, paperwork and working across time zones are not known for lowering it.

What that guideline recommends most strongly is something else: education and self-care, supervised jaw and postural exercise, manual therapy, and cognitive behavioural therapy.

## Where chiropractic fits, without overselling it

Within that combined approach, what we can contribute is work on the cervical spine and on the muscles of the neck and jaw. Here is what the trials say:

- A [meta-analysis in *Pain Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1093/pm/pnaa021) concluded that manual therapy to the neck reduces pain intensity more than placebo or minimal intervention in people with TMD, and that treating neck and jaw together beats treating the neck alone in the short term.
- A [review focused on the upper neck](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arrct.2022.100242), covering eight trials and 437 participants, found improvements in pain and mouth opening, but its authors describe the benefit as limited and the data as too mixed for firm conclusions.
- The effect of manual therapy [tends to fade over time](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm9113404) unless it is paired with exercise.

In plain terms: it is a reasonable thing to add, particularly if you also have [neck pain and headaches](/en/symptoms/neck-and-headache-pain/), but the evidence is modest and mostly short term. Most of those trials were also delivered by physiotherapists, not chiropractors. It does not replace your dentist, and we will not promise that the pain goes away. If a few visits bring no change, the honest move is to rethink the plan or refer you on.

## Self-checks and simple relief

None of this is a diagnosis, but it will help you describe what is going on, which is useful when the appointment is in your second language:

- **Are your teeth touching right now?** At rest they should be apart; [they only need to meet when you eat](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/temporomandibular-disorder-tmd/). If you catch yourself clenching several times a day, your bruxism is not only a night-time thing.
- **Open slowly in front of a mirror.** Does the jaw drift to one side? Does it hurt, or only click?
- **Press on the cheek just in front of the angle of the jaw, then on the temple.** Does that reproduce your usual pain?
- **Turn and tilt your neck.** Does the feeling in your jaw change?
- **When is it worst?** On waking suggests night-time activity; mid-afternoon suggests daytime clenching.

For relief, the advice from the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/temporomandibular-disorder-tmd/) and the NIDCR lines up: soft food for a few days, a warm or cold pack wrapped in a cloth (heat for 15 to 20 minutes, twice a day), gentle massage of the sore muscles, no chewing gum, pens or nails, no wide yawns, and some form of relaxation practice. A phone reminder that just says "teeth apart" is one of the few things studied for daytime clenching; the [evidence is limited](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-07856-z) but leans in favour of behavioural approaches like this.

One reassuring fact: many of these episodes [are short-lived and settle on their own](https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/tmd).

## When to see a dentist first

Start with a dentist, before us, if:

- You can see wear, chipped teeth or fillings that keep breaking.
- Your partner hears you grinding at night.
- The pain sits in one tooth, or flares with hot and cold.
- Your bite feels different and your teeth no longer meet the way they did.
- Your jaw locks, or you cannot open fully.
- You already wear a guard and it causes pain.

Three words will get you through the conversation at a Spanish practice: *bruxismo* (grinding or clenching), *ATM* (the joint) and *férula de descarga* (night guard).

Start with the neck if the jaw is a nuisance but stiffness and headaches are the main complaint, or if the dental side is already handled and the pain persists. Often the sensible answer is both, with each professional knowing what the other is doing.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can bruxism cause neck pain?**
They often occur together, and there is a plausible anatomical reason: muscles that work in coordination and shared nerve pathways. What studies have not shown is that one causes the other. It is worth having both assessed.

**Should I stop wearing my night guard if I see a chiropractor?**
No. The guard protects your teeth, and no adjustment does that. If it causes pain or you feel your bite changing, raise it with your dentist instead of quietly giving up on it.

**Will a neck adjustment get rid of my jaw pain?**
It may reduce it in the short term for some people, according to trials of manual therapy to the neck. The evidence is modest and there is no guarantee. It works better alongside exercise and self-care.

**How long does it take to improve?**
Many episodes ease by themselves fairly quickly. Pain lasting more than three months counts as chronic, and at that stage the best-supported approach is a combination of education, exercise, manual therapy and stress management, not any single measure.
