A **trigger point** is a small, hard, very tender spot inside a tight muscle: the classic "knot". What sets it apart is that pressing it **hurts on the spot and somewhere else too**, sometimes several centimetres away. They're very common in the back, almost always from overload or sustained postures, and most release with pressure, heat and movement.

## Symptoms

- A **knot** you can feel, inside a tight band of muscle.
- **Deep, dull pain** that's hard to point to with one finger.
- **Referred pain**: pressing the spot reproduces your pain or spreads it to a familiar area.
- Sometimes a small **twitch** in the muscle when pressed.
- **Stiffness** and less movement, worse with cold, stress or after staying still.

## Where they appear in the back, and where they send pain

| Muscle | Where the knot is | Where it can hurt |
|---|---|---|
| Upper trapezius | Between neck and shoulder | Side of the neck, temple, behind the eye |
| Levator scapulae | Where the neck meets the shoulder blade | Neck when turning, edge of the shoulder blade |
| Rhomboids | Between the spine and shoulder blade | Between the shoulder blades |
| Quadratus lumborum | Either side of the lower back | Lower back, hip, buttock |
| Gluteus medius | Upper outer buttock | Low back, buttock, outer thigh |

That's why pain between your shoulder blades or in your hip can come from a different muscle from the one that hurts.

## Why they appear

- **Sustained postures**: hours at a computer, driving, your phone.
- **Overexertion** or a repeated movement (lifting, training more than usual).
- **Stress and poor sleep**, which keep muscle tone high.
- **Guarding** after an injury or joint pain: the muscle tightens to protect the area and stays that way.

An honest note: trigger points are a useful and widely used clinical idea, but studies show that different practitioners don't always agree on where they are, and the exact mechanism is still being researched. In practice, treating them usually brings relief, especially short term, and what stops them coming back is moving more and changing what causes them.

## How to release one at home

1. **Sustained pressure with a ball** (tennis or massage ball) between your back and a wall: find the spot, lean in until the discomfort is tolerable (about 5-6 out of 10) and hold for **60-90 seconds**, until you feel it ease.
2. **Heat** for 15-20 minutes before or after.
3. **Gently stretch** the muscle, 30 seconds, pain-free.
4. **Move**: walk, roll your shoulders, change position every 30-45 minutes.
5. If it keeps coming back, **strengthen** that muscle and the ones around it.

Don't press on the bony points of the spine, press hard over the kidneys, or on areas that are inflamed or have varicose veins or wounds.

## How we treat it at the clinic

We find the trigger point and, above all, **why it's there**: a spinal joint that isn't moving well, a work posture, a shoulder that's compensating. Treatment combines:

- **Pressure and soft-tissue release** on the spot.
- **Gentle adjustments and mobilisation** of stiff areas of the spine that overload that muscle.
- **Exercises** and changes to your day so it doesn't come back.

## When it isn't a trigger point: see a doctor

Don't assume it's muscular if your back pain:

- Comes with **chest pain or tightness, breathlessness or a cold sweat** (call 112).
- Comes with **fever**, chills or pain when passing urine (it may be the kidney).
- Wakes you at night and **nothing changes it**.
- Comes with unexplained **weight loss** or a history of cancer.
- Started after a **knock or fall**.
- Comes with tingling or **weakness** in your arms or legs.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a trigger point the same as a muscle knot or contracture?**
They're related. A contracture is a generally tight muscle; a trigger point is a specific spot inside it that, when pressed, reproduces the pain and sometimes sends it elsewhere.

**How long does it take to release?**
Many ease within a few days with pressure, heat and movement. If one keeps coming back, look for what's maintaining it.

**Does treatment hurt?**
There's tolerable discomfort during the pressure, usually followed by relief. It shouldn't be strong pain.

**Does dry needling work?**
Reviews show short-term relief, similar to other manual techniques. It isn't essential.

**Why do I always get them in the same place?**
It's usually the muscle that works hardest in your posture or activity: the trapezius on your mouse side, or the quadratus lumborum if you sit for long hours.
